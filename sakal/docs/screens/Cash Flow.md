A cash flow report (or cash flow statement) is one of the three major financial statements, along with the Income Statement and Balance Sheet.

Its main purpose is to answer a very practical question:

“Where did the company's cash come from, and where did the cash go during a specific period?”

This is different from asking whether the company made a profit.

1. Why do we need a cash flow report?

Imagine this company:

ABC Trading LLC

During August:

Sales = AED 1,000,000
Expenses = AED 700,000
Profit = AED 300,000

It looks excellent.

But suppose customers have not yet paid AED 500,000 of those sales.

The company may have AED 300,000 profit on paper, but it might actually have very little cash available to pay:

salaries
suppliers
rent
loans
taxes
other expenses

This is why:

Profit ≠ Cash

A company can be profitable but have a cash shortage.

Conversely, a company can show a loss but still have significant cash available.

2. What exactly is cash flow?

Cash flow means the actual movement of cash and cash equivalents into and out of the business.

There are two directions:

Cash inflow

Money coming into the business.

Examples:

Customer payments
Loan received
Investment from owner
Sale of equipment
Interest received
Cash outflow

Money leaving the business.

Examples:

Supplier payments
Employee salaries
Rent
Purchase of equipment
Loan repayment
Dividends

The basic calculation is:

Net Cash Flow = Cash Inflows − Cash Outflows

3. The three major sections of a cash flow statement

A cash flow statement is generally divided into:

Operating Activities
Investing Activities
Financing Activities

You can remember this as:

Business operations → Assets/investments → Funding

4. Operating Cash Flow

Operating activities represent cash generated or consumed by the company's normal day-to-day business operations.

For a trading company, this could include:

Cash inflows
Cash received from customers
Collection of receivables
Other operating income
Cash outflows
Payments to suppliers
Salaries
Rent
Utilities
Insurance
Operating taxes
Other business expenses
Example

Suppose during August:

Operating Activity Cash
Customer collections +AED 900,000
Supplier payments -AED 500,000
Salaries -AED 150,000
Rent -AED 50,000
Utilities -AED 20,000
Other expenses -AED 30,000
Net Operating Cash Flow +AED 150,000

So the company generated:

AED 150,000 cash from its normal business activities.

This is usually the most important section for evaluating whether the core business is actually generating cash.

5. Investing Cash Flow

Investing activities are usually related to long-term assets and investments.

Examples:

Cash outflows
Purchase of computers
Purchase of vehicles
Purchase of machinery
Purchase of property
Purchase of long-term investments
Cash inflows
Sale of vehicles
Sale of equipment
Sale of property
Sale of investments
Example

ABC Trading purchases:

Delivery vehicle = AED 120,000
New computers = AED 30,000

And sells an old vehicle for:

AED 20,000

Then:

Investing Activity Cash
Purchase vehicle -AED 120,000
Purchase computers -AED 30,000
Sale old vehicle +AED 20,000
Net Investing Cash Flow -AED 130,000

The negative amount does not necessarily mean something bad.

The company may be investing in future growth.

6. Financing Cash Flow

Financing activities show how the company is funded and how it returns money to owners/lenders.

Examples include:

Cash inflows
Bank loan received
New shares issued
Owner capital introduced
Cash outflows
Loan repayment
Dividend payment
Share buyback
Capital returned to owners
Example

ABC Trading:

Takes a bank loan = AED 500,000
Repays existing loan = AED 150,000
Pays dividend = AED 50,000

Then:

Financing Activity Cash
New bank loan +AED 500,000
Loan repayment -AED 150,000
Dividend -AED 50,000
Net Financing Cash Flow +AED 300,000 7. Putting all three sections together

Now let's combine the example.

Section Net Cash Flow
Operating Activities +AED 150,000
Investing Activities -AED 130,000
Financing Activities +AED 300,000
Net Change in Cash +AED 320,000

So the company increased its cash by:

AED 320,000

8. Beginning cash and ending cash

The cash flow statement normally reconciles beginning cash to ending cash.

For example:

Beginning Cash

AED 200,000

Net Increase in Cash

AED 320,000

=

Ending Cash

AED 520,000

So:

Ending Cash = Beginning Cash + Net Change in Cash

This ending amount should generally reconcile with the relevant cash and cash-equivalent balances reported on the balance sheet.

9. Very important: Profit vs Cash Flow

This is where many people initially get confused.

Suppose you sell goods for AED 100,000 on credit.

You record:

Sales Revenue = AED 100,000

Suppose the goods cost you AED 60,000.

You might report:

Profit = AED 40,000

But the customer hasn't paid yet.

So:

Cash received = AED 0

Therefore:

Item Amount
Revenue AED 100,000
Cost AED 60,000
Profit AED 40,000
Cash received from customer AED 0

You made a profit but did not yet receive the cash.

This is one of the most important concepts in accounting.

10. Example of how receivables affect cash flow

Suppose:

Opening Accounts Receivable = AED 200,000

During the month:

Credit sales = AED 500,000

Customer payments = AED 350,000

Then:

Ending Accounts Receivable:

200,000 + 500,000 − 350,000 = AED 350,000

The company recorded AED 500,000 sales, but only collected AED 350,000.

So the cash flow from customers is based on the AED 350,000 collected, not the AED 500,000 sales.

11. Direct vs indirect method

There are two common ways of presenting cash flow from operating activities.

Direct method

It shows actual cash receipts and payments.

For example:

Cash received from customers 900,000
Cash paid to suppliers (500,000)
Cash paid to employees (150,000)
Cash paid for rent (50,000)
Cash paid for utilities (20,000)

---

Net operating cash flow 180,000

This is very easy for management to understand because it shows actual cash movement.

12. Indirect method

The indirect method starts with net profit and adjusts it to arrive at operating cash flow.

For example:

Net Profit 300,000

Add: Depreciation 50,000
Add: Increase in Accounts Payable 30,000
Less: Increase in Receivables (80,000)
Less: Increase in Inventory (40,000)

---

Net Operating Cash Flow 260,000

Why do we add depreciation?

Because depreciation is an expense in accounting, but no cash actually leaves the company when depreciation is recorded.

So it reduces accounting profit but does not represent a current-period cash outflow.

13. Why depreciation is added back

Suppose:

Company buys a machine for:

AED 100,000

The company pays the AED 100,000 cash when the machine is purchased.

But accounting might recognize depreciation:

AED 20,000 per year

That AED 20,000:

reduces accounting profit
but does not mean AED 20,000 cash left the company that year

The actual cash outflow occurred when the machine was purchased.

Therefore, under the indirect method:

Depreciation is added back.

14. Working capital and cash flow

Working capital is extremely important when analyzing cash flow.

Common items include:

Accounts Receivable
Inventory
Accounts Payable
Accounts Receivable increases

Usually means:

Customers owe us more money.

That generally reduces cash flow.

Inventory increases

Usually means:

We bought more inventory and haven't sold it yet.

That generally reduces cash flow.

Accounts Payable increases

Usually means:

We have not yet paid some suppliers.

That generally increases cash flow temporarily.

A useful rule for the indirect method:

Change Effect on Operating Cash
Receivables ↑ Cash ↓
Receivables ↓ Cash ↑
Inventory ↑ Cash ↓
Inventory ↓ Cash ↑
Payables ↑ Cash ↑
Payables ↓ Cash ↓ 15. Example of a complete cash flow statement

Let's build a simple example.

OPERATING ACTIVITIES

Cash paid to suppliers (500,000)
Cash paid to employees (150,000)

Cash paid for utilities (20,000)

---

Cash at Beginning of Period 150,000

Cash at End of Period 520,000 16. How management uses a cash flow report

A cash flow report is extremely useful for management.

For example, management can determine:

Can we pay salaries?

Suppose:

Cash balance = AED 500,000
Payroll next week = AED 450,000

The business may need to be careful even if it reports a large accounting profit.

Can we buy a new vehicle?

If operating cash flow is strong, the company may be able to fund the purchase.

Do we need a bank loan?

If operating cash flow is consistently negative, the company may need external financing.

Are customers paying on time?

A rapidly increasing Accounts Receivable balance can indicate a collection problem.

Are we generating cash from the core business?

This is one of the most important questions.

17. How to analyze a cash flow statement

Don't just look at the final cash balance.

Look at each section.

A. Operating cash flow

Ask:

Is the core business generating cash?

Ideally, a healthy mature business should generally generate positive operating cash flow over time.

For example:

Operating Cash Flow
2024 +500K
2025 +650K
2026 +800K

This is generally encouraging.

But:

2024 +800K
2025 +300K
2026 -200K

requires investigation.

18. Positive cash flow doesn't always mean healthy business

This is another important point.

Suppose:

Operating Cash Flow -200,000
Investing Cash Flow -50,000
Financing Cash Flow +1,000,000

Net cash:

+750,000

Looks good.

But why?

Because the company borrowed AED 1 million.

The company isn't necessarily generating cash from operations.

So you should always ask:

Where did the cash come from?

19. Negative cash flow doesn't always mean bad business

Suppose:

Operating Cash Flow +1,000,000
Investing Cash Flow -2,000,000
Financing Cash Flow +500,000

Net cash:

-500,000

At first glance this looks negative.

But perhaps the company spent AED 2 million buying:

a new factory
vehicles
machinery
technology infrastructure

That could be a strategic investment for growth.

Therefore, negative cash flow can sometimes be intentional and healthy.

20. Free Cash Flow

Another important metric related to cash flow is:

Free Cash Flow (FCF)

A commonly used simplified formula is:

Free Cash Flow = Operating Cash Flow − Capital Expenditure

For example:

Operating Cash Flow = AED 1,000,000

Capital Expenditure = AED 300,000

Therefore:

FCF = AED 700,000

This tells you roughly how much cash remains after the company has funded its capital investments.

FCF is widely used for:

valuation
investment analysis
debt repayment analysis
dividend analysis
management decisions 21. Cash flow report vs income statement vs balance sheet

These three reports answer different questions.

Report Main Question
Income Statement Did we make a profit?
Balance Sheet What do we own and owe?
Cash Flow Statement Where did our cash come from and go?

Think of a company like a person:

Income Statement

How much did I earn this month?

Balance Sheet

What assets do I have and what debts do I owe?

Cash Flow Statement

How much actual money came into and went out of my bank account?

22. A practical business example

Suppose a company has this situation:

But:

24. Cash flow forecasting

There is also an important distinction between:

Cash Flow Statement

and

Cash Flow Forecast

A cash flow statement looks at what already happened.

A cash flow forecast looks at what is expected to happen.

For example:

Historical

August actual:

Customer collections 900K
Supplier payments -500K
Payroll -150K
Net cash 250K
Forecast

September expected:

Expected collections 950K
Expected supplier pay. -600K
Expected payroll -160K
Expected loan payment -100K
Expected net cash 90K

A finance department may use forecasts to determine whether there will be enough cash in the coming weeks or months.

26. One very important concept

A good finance person does not only ask:

“How much profit did we make?”

They also ask:

“How much cash did we generate?”

And then:

“Why is there a difference between profit and cash?”

That difference is often explained by:

receivables
inventory
payables
depreciation
accruals
asset purchases
loans
other non-cash accounting adjustments

That is essentially the heart of cash-flow analysis.
